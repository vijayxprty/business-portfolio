CASE STUDY — EVENTRY

Every event,
one ticket.

Eventry is a website for selling event tickets without the faff — find the event, see the details, get the ticket. I designed it end to end: a dark hero with one real event in a phone, a create-or-sign-in split, and a ticket card that carries the date, the price and the buy button in one glance.

02 / THE BRIEF

Selling tickets meant
a post and a spreadsheet.

For a small event, selling tickets meant a social post, a spreadsheet and chasing payments by hand. Eventry gives every event its own page — create it, invite people, sell the tickets — so the organiser stops being the box office.

The problem

Small events live on social posts and group chats. The organiser posts, people reply “I’m in”, and then someone has to collect the money, track the numbers and answer the same three questions fifty times. The event fills up — or it doesn’t — and nobody knows until the day.

Eventry turns that into one page: create the event, invite the attendees, sell the tickets. My job was to make the first screen sell that idea in five seconds — to organisers who create, and to attendees who just want a ticket.

What I did

  • Dark hero, one real event — a phone holding a dog course, not a stock photo
  • Create vs sign in — two buttons, two audiences, no third option
  • Event card that sells — date, time, price and a buy button in one glance
  • Trust in the footer — Terms, Privacy and Security on the homepage itself
  • Swedish where it counts — the ticket moment in the buyer’s own language
  • One viewport, whole pitch — everything above the fold, nothing to hunt for
03 / FROM WIREFRAME TO SCREEN

Drawn in the dark.
Green did the talking.

These wireframes were drawn in the site’s own colours — dark charcoal, white type, one mint green for every action. If a screen didn’t read in dark, it didn’t read at all.

The wireframes settled three decisions early: the hero sells one event, not the platform. Discover is a search bar and a grid of cards — no categories page in between. And the ticket page keeps the event card on screen while you pay, so you never wonder what you’re buying.

04 / WHAT THE RESEARCH SHOWED

Tested with real people.
Changed by what they did.

Before the final UI, I ran the flows past first-time users and watched where they hesitated. Four findings shaped the finished page.

01

The event is the hero

In testing, people tapped the phone first — a dog course, 500 Kr, 25 April, a green buy button. So the hero sells one concrete event instead of the platform in the abstract. The phone is the demo, the headline is the promise, and the two never fight each other.

02

Two buttons, two jobs

Create an Event in white, Sign In in outline. First-time visitors split cleanly — organisers create, attendees sign in — and nobody asked what the difference was. A third button was tried in an early round and dropped: it only ever stole clicks from the other two.

03

Swedish where it counts

The shell is in English, but the ticket moment speaks Swedish — Valpkurs, Köpbiljetter, 500 Kr. In testing, Swedish buyers trusted the card the moment they saw their own words on it, and the price in kronor needed no converting in their heads.

04

The footer does the trusting

Terms, Privacy and Security sit one line under Discover, Pricing and Help — on the homepage itself, not buried three clicks deep. First-time buyers scrolled straight there before paying, so the trust links live where the buying decision happens.
